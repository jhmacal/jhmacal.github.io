The European Union AI Act

Did Omnibus pause obligations for high-risk AI?

By Julio Macedo

What the Act is

The European Union's Artificial Intelligence Act, formally Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, is the first comprehensive horizontal artificial intelligence law adopted by a major jurisdiction. Its risk-based architecture sorts AI systems into four categories. Unacceptable-risk systems are banned outright, with narrow exemptions. High-risk systems, listed in Annex III, trigger the Act's core obligation stack: risk management, data governance, technical documentation, record-keeping, transparency, human oversight, accuracy, and robustness. Limited-risk systems carry transparency obligations only. Minimal-risk systems are unregulated under the Act. A separate category covers general-purpose AI models, including the foundation models that power most consumer-facing generative AI products.

The Act in Seven Moves

From Commission proposal to where we are now

21 April 2021

The Commission proposes a horizontal AI regulation

The European Commission publishes its proposal for a Regulation laying down harmonised rules on artificial intelligence. The proposal introduces the risk-based architecture that will survive the legislative process largely intact: unacceptable, high, limited, and minimal risk, with conformity assessment and CE-marking modelled on existing product-safety regimes. The proposal is the first attempt by a major jurisdiction to legislate AI horizontally rather than sector by sector.

sources: Regulation (EU) 2024/1689; Council and Parliament trilogue records

The Act applies to providers placing AI systems on the European Union market, to deployers established or located in the Union, and to providers and deployers whose AI system output is used in the Union. The territorial scope captures any global firm whose AI activity touches a European customer, employee, or counterparty. A United States bank running a credit-scoring model that produces decisions for a European subsidiary's customers is inside the perimeter. A global investment manager using an AI hiring tool that screens candidates in Frankfurt is inside the perimeter. The scope is broader than the headline category labels suggest, and the high-risk classifications under Annex III map directly to financial services activities: creditworthiness and credit scoring, biometric identification, employment systems, and other categories where the obligation stack will eventually attach.

The Act follows the European Union's product-safety regulatory tradition: classify by risk, impose proportional obligations, require conformity assessment, register, monitor, sanction. The mechanism is familiar to anyone who has worked with medical devices, machinery, or chemicals under EU law. AI is the first general-purpose digital technology Brussels has chosen to regulate horizontally, and the regulatory approach will set the operational reference for AI governance well beyond the European market.

Each stage of the process matters operationally. The 2021 proposal set the architecture. The 2022 and 2023 negotiations added the general-purpose AI chapter, which did not exist in the original Commission text and was added in response to the public release of ChatGPT in November 2022. The May 2024 Council adoption made the Act binding European Union law. The July 2024 Official Journal publication started the clock on the staggered application calendar that the Act itself sets out. The 2024 and 2025 market reaction produced both the AI Pact (a voluntary compliance forum) and the standards-development work at CEN-CENELEC that the Act's compliance regime depends on. The Brussels Effect, the phenomenon by which European Union regulation becomes the global operational reference, has begun to take hold for AI in the same way it did for personal data after the General Data Protection Regulation took effect in 2018. And the May 2026 Digital Omnibus political agreement deferred the high-risk obligations by sixteen months, from August 2026 to December 2027.

The Brussels Effect on AI

The General Data Protection Regulation entered into force in May 2018 and produced an effect that Anu Bradford named the year before: when European Union regulation of a digital activity is the strictest in the world, the cost of regional fragmentation for a global firm exceeds the cost of compliance with the European regime, and the European regime becomes the firm's global standard. The General Data Protection Regulation became the de facto privacy operating reference for global firms with European operations within eighteen months of taking effect. The same dynamic has begun for AI.

A global investment bank with European subsidiaries, a global asset manager with European customers, or a global technology firm with European users cannot reasonably maintain two AI governance architectures: a stricter one inside the European perimeter and a looser one outside it. The cost of running two regimes, the audit complexity of explaining to any supervisor why two standards exist for the same technology, and the reputational cost of governing AI to different standards in different jurisdictions all exceed the cost of building the function once to the stricter regime and operating it globally. Large regulated institutions with European exposure face that calculus.

"The companies that will be in the strongest and most defensible position by the time the new deadlines apply are those that use this additional time strategically." Dastra, advising clients after the May 2026 Omnibus agreement.

Other jurisdictions reference the European Union's approach when developing their own AI regimes. The United Kingdom's pro-innovation framework, Singapore's Model AI Governance Framework, Brazil's AI bill (which adopted a risk-based structure modelled on the European text), and the United States' sector-by-sector guidance under the Federal Reserve, the Office of the Comptroller of the Currency, and other regulators all sit in conversation with the European regime. A global firm that builds to satisfy the European Union's standards has, in practice, built to satisfy most of what the other regimes will ask for. A firm that builds to satisfy only its home regulator pays twice.

The Omnibus delay, accurately read

On 7 May 2026, in the third political trilogue under the Cypriot Presidency, the Council of the European Union and the European Parliament reached provisional political agreement on the Digital Omnibus on AI. The agreement defers the application of the Act's high-risk obligations: standalone Annex III high-risk systems now apply from 2 December 2027, and high-risk AI systems embedded in Annex I regulated products apply from 2 August 2028. The Article 50(2) watermarking and transparency obligations for generative AI providers move from 2 August 2026 to 2 December 2026, a four-month grace rather than the six the Commission originally proposed. The provisional agreement is subject to formal adoption by the European Parliament and the Council. The Official Journal has not yet published. Until it does, the original deadlines remain the legally binding ones.

The legislators were explicit about why the delay was granted. The Hogan Lovells advisory of May 2026 quotes the official framing directly: "acknowledges that AI Act compliance requires significant preparation and the introduction of supporting technical standards. The delay is intended to provide businesses with additional time to achieve compliance, while underscoring the expectation that implementation efforts should already be underway." Dastra's client advisory of the same period states the point even more plainly: "The extension of certain deadlines under the Omnibus should not be interpreted as an invitation to pause AI governance efforts until 2027." Modulos, advising compliance teams the week of the agreement, was blunter still: "If you paused your AI Act preparation on the assumption that Brussels would keep moving the goalposts, the goalposts have stopped moving."

Three independent advisories naming the same misread suggest the misread is widespread. The shape of the misread is the same in each: firms reading the delay as a pause, recalibrating their internal timelines downward, and producing the late-stage scramble the legislators were trying to avoid. The supervisor reads what an institution operated during 2026 and 2027.

The Article 4 AI literacy obligation has been applicable since 2 February 2025. It requires providers and deployers of AI systems to ensure a sufficient level of AI literacy among staff and contractors handling those systems. The obligation is proportionate to the role, the context, and the system. It requires a documented training program with role-appropriate content for every employee or contractor whose work touches AI. Most firms with firmwide AI deployments are already past the threshold at which the obligation attaches to thousands of people simultaneously. A firm cannot produce the documentation regime that proves compliance after the fact.

The Article 5 prohibitions on unacceptable AI practices have been in force since the same February 2025 date. The general-purpose AI provider obligations entered into application on 2 August 2025, with Commission enforcement powers attaching from 2 August 2026. Neither was delayed by the Omnibus. The Article 111(3) legacy carve-out gives providers of general-purpose models placed on the market before 2 August 2025 until 2 August 2027 to comply. The Article 50 transparency obligations that apply to deployers (the obligations to inform users when they are interacting with an AI system, when they are seeing content that has been generated or modified by AI, or when they are subject to an emotion-recognition or biometric-categorisation system) continue to apply from August 2026 as the Act originally provided. None of those moved.

What the supervisor reads without the AI Act

The AI Act is not the only regulatory regime under which a supervisor engages with a regulated institution's AI governance. Four standing regimes apply today, none of them dependent on the AI Act's timeline.

The General Data Protection Regulation has been in force since May 2018. Article 22 governs solely automated decision-making with legal or similarly significant effect, which is what credit decisioning, certain candidate-screening systems, and other regulated AI outputs produce. Article 35 requires a Data Protection Impact Assessment for high-risk processing, and the European Data Protection Board and national data protection authorities have been engaging on automated decision-making and on AI training data continuously since the Regulation took effect. None of this is delayed. None of it depends on the AI Act.

The European Banking Authority's Guidelines on internal governance require regulated credit institutions to maintain robust governance arrangements over all material activities. An AI deployment operating at firmwide scale across a bank is a material activity by any reasonable reading. The supervisor can examine the governance of that activity today under the existing internal-governance framework. The European Banking Authority's Guidelines on outsourcing arrangements require third-party risk management for any function that relies on external providers, which captures every firmwide AI tool that integrates externally-provided foundation models. The Digital Operational Resilience Act has been applicable since 17 January 2025 and governs information and communication technology risk, third-party ICT risk management, and operational resilience for financial entities. AI systems sit inside the perimeter as ICT services.

The European Central Bank's published supervisory priorities for 2026 through 2028, released 11 November 2025, name banks' AI strategies, governance, and risk management as a medium-term strategic focus across the three-year cycle. That document is the supervisor announcing where it intends to look. The announcement is not contingent on the AI Act timeline. The supervisor's authority to examine an institution's AI governance arrangements during 2026 and 2027 derives from the standing regimes already in force.

Major global banks already have to operate the function under the standing regimes. What the supervisor reads in 2027 is what the institution has been operating during 2026. Start in mid-2027 and there is nothing for the supervisor to examine in early 2028. Start in 2025 and the documentation arrives by itself.

The work is the same regardless of when the Act bites. Model-risk inventory. Decision-rights matrix across Legal, Compliance, Risk, Engineering, and the business line. Contractual allocation across the AI value chain (Article 25). Data-flow regime against GDPR Article 5 and Act Article 10. Incident-response playbook. AI literacy program by role. Each one has a non-AI-Act predecessor under the standing regimes.

The Omnibus did not buy the market sixteen months. The Article 111(3) carve-out extends to August 2027 for grandfathered general-purpose AI models. The standing stack continues to engage everyone else. Article 4 literacy and Article 5 prohibitions have been applicable since February 2025. GDPR Article 22 governs automated decision-making with legal effect. The EBA Guidelines on internal governance and outsourcing cover credit institutions today. DORA covers ICT risk and operational resilience today. The ECB named banks' AI strategies, governance, and risk management a medium-term strategic focus for 2026 through 2028.
