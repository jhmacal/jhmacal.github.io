Enablement & Teaching

Prompt Engineering (O'Mono)

A reusable API-powered prompt-engineering application that exposes field-level rationale, functioning as embedded training and self-service enablement, endowing AI literacy that compounds and scales.

Click here to advance to Enterprise Update

By Julio Macedo

An associate types a sentence about a training session she must run on Thursday, and seconds later reads why the system decided her prompt needed a Role, why it drafted an example but refused to include it, and what rule she could carry into the next prompt she writes.

Sixty-nine percent of legal professionals now use generative AI at work, and fifty-four percent of their firms provide no training and have no plans to provide any.1 Asked what they expect from new lawyers, seventy-six percent of practicing attorneys named cite-checking AI output and fourteen percent named prompt engineering, which leaves the skill that decides what the model produces near the bottom of what the profession has agreed to require.2 Tools like O'Mono live in that gap.

1. The Final Product

Eight fields come back from a single sentence of input, two of them deliberately left out of the prompt itself, each one carrying a verdict on whether it belongs, a reason written for this input, and a rule of thumb built to travel.

The reason expires with the input that produced it. The rule of thumb is registered on the machine and outlives both, since it is written for somebody who no longer has the application in front of them.

With her own prompt in front of her, the associate reads that once and learns what a training module could have told her in the abstract and never made stick.

The application drafts an example, decides against including it, and shows her the draft anyway, with the reason for leaving it out and the rule of thumb that follows from it.

Above the field level sits the construction strategy for the whole prompt, and a toggle appends all of it, strategy and verdicts and rules of thumb together, to whatever gets copied. The teaching travels wherever the prompt travels, to the colleague who receives it and into the matter file where it lands.

2. Data

The average conversation with a general AI tool runs two prompts, while the heaviest five percent of users average eighteen.3 Two prompts is a question, a disappointing answer, and a person deciding the tool is not for them. Nobody files a ticket to report that, so the department never learns it happened.

I designed and redesigned O'Mono repeatedly to find out whether the enablement layer could carry more weight, and what follows is what the building taught me. The record here exists because I kept meeting the same corrections and had no way to tell which ones they were. A recurrence a person can feel but cannot name is not yet a curriculum.

Every prompt it generates stores its rules of thumb, and months of those entries, read together, describe what one person keeps getting wrong.

Each entry carries a direction, and the entry at the foot of that view shows a rule marked gone, meaning it stopped appearing partway through the record and has not come back, which is the closest thing to evidence that somebody learned it.

Two phrasings of one rule fold into a single entry, since a view that counted them separately would measure vocabulary where it means to measure habit. The horizon runs to the last prompt in the log, not to today, so that a week away from the application cannot present itself as progress.

That record is mine, and what it changed is the part I can vouch for directly. My prompts now carry the parts I used to leave out. I number the tasks so the model does not skip one, I name a role when the work is specific enough that the answer depends on who gives it, and I supply the fields the model treats loosely before it asks for them. None of that arrived in a course. It came from reading, on my own work, why a field was judged weak, one prompt at a time.

A department that has run this for a year holds something no vendor can sell: a dated account of what its own people found hard, what they stopped finding hard, and roughly when the change happened.

2.1 The Enterprise's Curriculum

In the enterprise version an administrator reaches only the central folder of reports, one file for each installation, and the page that merges them states its limits before it states a number, so the page cannot be opened onto a person by any setting:

From the prose a prompt produces, only the rules of thumb leave the user's machine, filed against the eight prompt fields: Role, Task, Context, Input, Constraints, Examples, Format and Tone. The export names the wording of the rule as a single literal path and refuses every other field against it. What leaves the machine is what somebody needed to learn, and never what they were working on.

Across an enterprise with hundreds of users and months of usage, the data becomes curriculum.

3. Privilege

A prompt carrying sensitive terms or data, or attaching a protected document or image, stops on the machine before any request leaves it, and the message names what it found in ordinary language rather than in a class code the reader would have to look up. The attorney can also explain to a client what happens to a document inside the application, showing it live.

The check runs with no network involved, because the machine is the only place a transmission can be prevented. Every block arrives with a way forward, since hard stops without an alternative are what push people into unapproved tools.

LayerX's State of AI Usage Report 2026 finds that nearly half of all enterprise AI conversations happen through personal identities rather than managed corporate accounts, and that over 14 percent of conversations on corporate identities are tied to personal AI licenses. Enterprise-shaped products like Copilot M365 and Gemini Enterprise are used mostly through managed accounts, while ChatGPT and Claude stay dominated by personal use even inside companies that bought the enterprise version. More than 6 percent of those conversations carry sensitive data.4 The constraint layer is not redundant in a firm with enterprise licenses: it is aimed at exactly the traffic the enterprise license does not cover.

Most of the AI Act obligations cited bind providers and deployers of high-risk systems specifically, while Article 4 binds providers and deployers of AI systems generally and has stood since 2 February 2025, untouched when the Digital Omnibus deferred the high-risk obligations to late 2027.5 The table shows that a control exists in the code and corresponds to a cited requirement, which is not the same as full compliance.

A general counsel who buys a monitoring product in November 2027 will have bought inspection, and inspection answers a different question from the one Article 4 asks. What Article 4 asks for is evidence that the people operating these systems understood what they were operating, and that evidence is a history of what a team was taught, week by week, out of the work it was doing.6 A history of that kind cannot be bought in the quarter a regulator asks for it, which is the argument for building the teaching layer first.

The professional rules did not move either. The ABA Model Rules bind lawyers rather than software, and no state bar's competence obligation changed when the European timetable did.

5. The Lesson

The teaching layer changed behavior, and the governance layer settled how much of the real work that teaching could reach. A tool trusted with a real matter gets opened on that matter, gets read while somebody is deciding something, and leaves a rule of thumb behind that outlives the session, while a tool nobody trusts with anything sensitive teaches people on the easy half of their job.

Numbering the tasks is decomposition, naming a role decides who the answer is for, saying what not to do is a constraint, and naming the jurisdiction is scope. None of that is a trick about wording, and none of it arrives with a better model, because it encodes what the person wanted and intent is the part a lawyer is paid for. Stronger models make a vague instruction cheaper to survive. They do not remove the need to say what you want, and a system that runs forty steps before anybody looks raises the cost of not saying it.

Somewhere around the end of next year a regulator or a client or a bar will ask a department what its people understood about these systems and when they understood it, and the answer will be a history that already exists or an assurance nobody can check.

O'Mono

Enterprise Update

The AI Act does not prescribe six curriculum modules. It defines AI literacy broadly as the skills, knowledge, and understanding needed to make informed AI deployment decisions and understand opportunities, risks, and possible harm. Article 4 requires the measures to account for the user's background, the context of use, and the people affected.

The central idea

Recognizing task-specific risks should be the center of the entire curriculum.

Competency The practical question O'Mono teaches

Competency1. Recognizing task-specific risks

Competency2. Understanding AI and its limits

Competency3. Reviewing and verifying outputs

The practical question O'Mono teachesWhat exactly must I check before relying on this result?

Competency4. Instructing AI effectively

The practical question O'Mono teachesHow do I give the system enough direction to perform the task properly?

Competency5. Selecting appropriate systems and uses

The practical question O'Mono teachesShould AI be used for this task, and which system is appropriate?

Competency6. Understanding human responsibility and affected persons

The practical question O'Mono teachesWho owns the decision, who could be affected, and who must review or intervene?

O'Mono teaches people how to recognize and control the risks of using AI for a specific work task.

1. Recognizing task-specific risks

This should drive everything.

O'Mono should teach the user to look at the actual task and identify the concrete ways AI could produce a bad outcome.

For legal research

  • Invented authorities.
  • Real authorities mischaracterized.
  • Outdated law.
  • Wrong jurisdiction.
  • Missing contrary authority.
  • Privileged information reaching the wrong system.

For a client email

  • Incorrect facts.
  • Overstating the legal conclusion.
  • Revealing internal analysis.
  • Creating unintended commitments.
  • Using the wrong tone with a sensitive recipient.

For spreadsheet analysis

  • Wrong column interpretation.
  • Hidden missing data.
  • Incorrect formulas.
  • Correlation presented as causation.
  • Confident conclusions from incomplete data.

The lesson:

This task asks the model to determine whether cases remain good law. This system cannot independently guarantee that. Each case must be checked in a current legal research source.

That is actual teaching.

2. Understanding AI and its limits

This should explain the reason behind the risk.

O'Mono does not need to teach transformer architecture or a technical AI course. It should teach only the limitation relevant to the current task. Examples:

  • The model generates probable language; it does not inherently know whether a statement is true.
  • The system may lack access to current information.
  • The model may not have opened or accurately read the cited source.
  • A long document may exceed what the model can meaningfully process.
  • The system may lose details across a long conversation.
  • A model with web access is different from one with access to an authoritative legal database.
  • A polished answer can still be incomplete.

The user should leave understanding:

This is why I cannot rely on this output in the same way I rely on the underlying source.

3. Reviewing and verifying outputs

This should teach what review means for this task. "Review the output" is meaningless unless O'Mono identifies the required review actions.

For a litigation draft

  • Open every authority.
  • Confirm the quotation.
  • Confirm the pincite.
  • Check treatment and subsequent history.
  • Check that the authority applies in the relevant jurisdiction.
  • Compare the completed document against the source record.
  • Look for authorities added after the verification process.

For a contract summary

  • Compare every stated obligation with the contract.
  • Confirm dates, notice periods, monetary amounts, defined terms, and exceptions.
  • Check whether the summary omitted provisions that change the apparent rule.
  • Separate express language from interpretation.

For data analysis

  • Recalculate key figures.
  • Confirm denominator and time period.
  • Inspect missing values.
  • Confirm the correct source columns were used.
  • Test whether the conclusion changes under another reasonable assumption.

Current O'Mono already contains part of this idea through its filing verification gate, although it records that the user checked the work rather than independently verifying that the work was correct.7

4. Instructing AI effectively

This is where the current O'Mono belongs most clearly.

The eight fields, Role, Task, Context, Input, Constraints, Examples, Format, and Tone, are methods for instructing AI. O'Mono already teaches through verdicts, explanations, and rules attached to those fields.7

The curriculum should teach questions such as:

  • Did I clearly state what I want done?
  • Did I separate multiple deliverables?
  • Did I provide the relevant facts and source materials?
  • Did I identify the jurisdiction, audience, date, or decision context?
  • Did I specify what the model must not assume?
  • Did I define what a successful answer includes?
  • Would an example help, or would it improperly anchor the answer?
  • Am I asking the model to do something it cannot actually do?

The eight fields are therefore retained. They become the internal structure of this competency rather than the entire literacy curriculum.

6. Understanding human responsibility and affected persons

The user should understand that AI assistance does not transfer responsibility to the system. O'Mono should ask:

  • Who is responsible for the final work?
  • Is this output merely supporting a person, or influencing a decision?
  • Who could be affected if it is wrong?
  • How serious would the consequences be?
  • Does the reviewer have sufficient expertise and authority?
  • Does another person need to approve the result?
  • Must the use be disclosed?
  • Is the affected person entitled to explanation, correction, appeal, or human reconsideration?

Examples:

  • A lawyer remains responsible for the filing.
  • A manager remains responsible for an employment decision.
  • A doctor remains responsible for clinical judgment.
  • A company remains responsible for a customer eligibility decision.
  • A recruiter remains responsible for how an applicant is evaluated.

This category prevents "human review" from becoming a meaningless checkbox.

O'Mono should use embedded coaching, not quizzes. It teaches while helping the user complete real work: warn about the likely failure, explain why, build the protection into the prompt, tell the user what to verify, and tell the user exactly what to bring back if it fails.

That fits O'Mono's existing architecture. It already has an intermediary interpretation phase, explains its construction decisions, lets the user edit the result, and records whether the output worked.7 The change is what the lesson contains. Instead of ending at a generic "rule of thumb," it should connect the task, risk, mitigation, verification, and recovery route. Current O'Mono already has the verdict, reason and lesson surface where this can live.7

The teaching unit

For any material issue, O'Mono should show this:

The teaching unit

Be careful: [How this type of task commonly fails.]

Why: [The relevant limitation of AI or this system.]

What I changed: [What O'Mono added to the prompt or workflow to reduce the risk.]

After you receive the answer: [What the user must verify.]

If it fails: [Exactly what the user should bring back to O'Mono.]

No answer is required. No test interrupts the workflow. The user can read it, skim it, or ignore it. The lesson still changes the prompt and remains available in the reasoning.

Full example

The user enters:

Find recent New York cases supporting enforcement of a customer non-solicitation provision and draft a short argument.

1. Intermediary phase

O'Mono interprets the task, then shows:

What matters for this task

Be careful: Legal research can fail even when the answer includes real-looking citations. A case may not exist, may not support the proposition stated, may no longer be good law, or may come from the wrong court. The model may also find supporting authority while missing contrary authority.

Why: A language model produces a plausible answer from the information and tools available to it. A citation in its answer is not proof that the authority exists, supports the proposition, or remains current.

What I am changing: I will require:

  • New York jurisdiction and a current-through date.
  • Separate supporting and contrary authority.
  • The exact proposition each case supports.
  • Court, year, citation, and source link.
  • A statement when an authority cannot be verified.
  • No claim that a case remains good law unless that status comes from an approved citator.

After you receive the answer: Open every authority in an approved legal research system. Check the proposition, quotation, court, date, and subsequent treatment.

If it fails: Return here and paste the citation together with the sentence that relied on it. If the problem is missing contrary authority, paste the answer and write: "Check for omitted contrary authority."

That teaches all six competencies without asking the user to prove anything:

  • Task-specific risks: invented, mischaracterized, outdated, or incomplete authority.
  • AI limits: plausible language is not validation.
  • System selection: legal database for authority status; language model for synthesis.
  • Instructing: jurisdiction, date, source requirements, contrary authority.
  • Verification: exact checks to perform.
  • Responsibility: the lawyer must validate the result before relying on it.

2. Reasoning phase

When O'Mono delivers the prompt, its reasoning says:

Why I built it this way

  • I added a current-through date because a case can accurately describe an earlier legal rule and still be unusable today.
  • I separated supporting and contrary authority because a request framed around "supporting enforcement" can cause the model to confirm the desired conclusion instead of testing it.
  • I required the proposition supported by each case because a citation can be real while the surrounding sentence overstates or misstates the holding.
  • I did not ask the model to certify that a case remains good law. That determination should come from a current legal research source with treatment information.
  • The final legal conclusion remains with the responsible attorney.

This is more useful than "rule of thumb: always verify citations." The user learns what can fail, why it fails, and what each prompt instruction is designed to prevent.

3. Final prompt

O'Mono produces:

You are assisting a New York attorney with preliminary legal research concerning the enforceability of a customer non-solicitation provision.

Identify recent, relevant New York authority current through August 4, 2026. Prioritize the New York Court of Appeals, the Appellate Division, and federal courts applying New York law.

Analyze:

  • Authorities supporting enforcement.
  • Authorities refusing or limiting enforcement.
  • The factors that materially distinguish the outcomes.
  • Any uncertainty or unresolved issue requiring additional research.

For every authority, provide:

  • Full citation.
  • Court and year.
  • The precise proposition for which the case is offered.
  • A short quotation or description of the relevant holding.
  • Whether the authority is binding or persuasive.
  • A link or source location when available.
  • Any limitation on relying on the authority.

Do not invent authorities, quotations, procedural history, or treatment. Do not state that an authority remains good law unless that status is established through an approved current citator. If an authority cannot be verified, clearly mark it "unverified."

Search for material contrary authority rather than presenting only cases supporting enforcement.

After the research table, draft a short argument based only on authorities identified in the table. Separate the authorities' holdings from your own analysis.

This output is research assistance. The responsible attorney must independently validate every authority and retain the final legal judgment.

The teaching appears in the reasoning, and the safeguards also operate inside the prompt.

4. After-the-answer instruction

The final screen should have a separate user-facing panel. This part is not copied into the model prompt.

Before relying on the answer

  • Find every case in an approved research source.
  • Confirm that the case says what the answer claims.
  • Check the quoted language and cited page.
  • Check negative treatment and subsequent history.
  • Confirm that the court and date fit the intended use.
  • Search independently for material contrary authority.

Bring it back if

  • A citation cannot be found.
  • A case does not support the stated proposition.
  • Negative treatment changes the analysis.
  • The answer omits relevant adverse authority.
  • The authorities are real but the final argument overstates them.

Paste only the citation, the questionable sentence, and any verified source passage necessary to diagnose the problem. The user now knows what failure looks like and how to recover from it.

What happens when the user returns

Suppose the answer cites a real case, but the case does not support the proposition. The user presses Fix this result and pastes:

AI answer: "Smith v. Jones establishes that customer non-solicitation provisions are routinely enforceable."

Verified source: The court declined to enforce the provision because it was geographically overbroad.

O'Mono already knows:

  • The original task.
  • Which lesson was shown.
  • Which safeguards were included.
  • Which model and system were selected.
  • Whether the user edited or removed a safeguard.
  • What the final prompt required.

O'Mono responds:

What failed

The authority appears to be real, but the answer overstated its holding. This is a proposition-support failure, not merely a missing citation.

Why the original safeguard was insufficient

The prompt required the model to state the proposition supported by each case, but the model still mischaracterized the decision.

What will reduce this failure

Use verified excerpts from the case as the source material. Ask the model to analyze only those excerpts rather than reconstructing the holding from memory or incomplete retrieval.

Next step

Paste the verified passage and any relevant surrounding text. O'Mono will rebuild the analysis prompt using the source language as the controlling input.

The revised prompt might say:

Using only the supplied verified excerpt, explain:

  • What the court held.
  • Why it declined to enforce the provision.
  • Which facts were material.
  • Whether the decision supports, limits, or contradicts the proposed argument.

Do not rely on knowledge outside the supplied excerpt.

This is teaching through diagnosis and repair.

How to interpret repeated returns

Repeated use of O'Mono means nothing by itself. The user may understand the lesson and still use the tool because it is convenient. The important signal is repeated preventable failure.

What happened What it likely means

What happenedThe lesson was shown, but the user removed the safeguard; the same failure returned

What it likely meansPossible learning gap

What happenedThe lesson was shown, the safeguard remained, and the model still failed

What it likely meansModel, system, or workflow problem

What happenedThe user returned because verification caught a problem

What it likely meansEvidence that the user followed the lesson

What happenedThe user repeatedly uses O'Mono but reports no failure

What it likely meansConvenience; no conclusion about learning

What happenedA different safeguard consistently prevents the failure

What it likely meansEvidence about what the curriculum should teach

What happenedMany users experience the same failure despite following instructions

What it likely meansOrganizational tool or process problem, not user illiteracy

How this builds a real curriculum

The curriculum is built from failure-and-repair patterns, not from accumulated slogans. Suppose the aggregate record shows:

  • 120 legal research tasks.
  • O'Mono warned about citation reliability in 95.
  • Users returned with 31 citation-related failures.
  • In 22, the citation existed but did not support the proposition.
  • Generic "provide citations" instructions did not reduce the failure.
  • Requiring exact source passages reduced the failure.
  • Using verified passages as input reduced it further.
  • Good-law problems still required an approved citator.

That produces a real curriculum module:

Module: why a real citation can still produce a false legal proposition

What users need to understand

  • Existence, proposition support, quotation accuracy, jurisdictional fit, and good-law status are different questions.
  • A citation requirement does not solve all five.
  • A general language model and a legal citator perform different functions.

What users should do

  • Require the proposition supported by each authority.
  • Open and read the source.
  • Compare the answer with the source language.
  • Use an approved citator for treatment.
  • Give the model verified source text when precision matters.

What the evidence showed

  • Asking for citations did not prevent mischaracterization.
  • Source-grounded analysis reduced it.
  • Current authority status still required a different system.

That teaches something substantive. It came from actual work, actual failures, and the measures that reduced those failures.

The final model

O'Mono teaches at four moments:

  • Before the prompt: "This type of task commonly fails in these ways."
  • With the reasoning: "I added these protections because each prevents a specific failure."
  • After the prompt: "Verify these things before relying on the result."
  • When something fails: "Bring back this specific material; I will diagnose the failure and change the workflow."

The resulting curriculum answers:

  • What types of work are failing?
  • Why are they failing?
  • Which warnings users receive?
  • Which protections reduce the failure?
  • Which failures remain even after users follow the guidance?
  • Which problems require education?
  • Which require a better model, system, workflow, or organizational control?

That is materially stronger than counting recurring rules of thumb.
