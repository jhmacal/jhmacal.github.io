Legal AI Daily Lesson 1

Legal AI Is Not One Thing

The problem is not that lawyers cannot understand legal AI. The problem is that people keep using one word for four different things. A model, a tool, a workflow, and governance are not the same object. Separate them and the room gets easier to read.

The Situation

The room says AI. That is not enough.

A partner says the firm is testing AI for contract review. A client asks whether legal can summarize intake questions with AI. A vendor says its platform has a legal reasoning engine. Each sentence sounds like one thing. It is not.

The lawyer move is to slow the room down and ask which layer everyone means: model, tool, workflow, or governance.

Core Concept

Legal AI usually means a stack of decisions. The model generates or classifies language. The tool gives lawyers a usable surface. The workflow decides where output goes. Governance decides whether the use is allowed at all.

Why It Matters
01

You stop asking vague questions.

“Is the AI safe?” is too broad. “What data reaches the model?” and “Who reviews the output before use?” are questions the room can answer.

02

You hear vendor demos differently.

A beautiful product screen can hide weak governance. A strong model can still sit inside a poor workflow. The layers keep the room honest.

03

You protect legal judgment.

Lawyers still carry competence, confidentiality, supervision, and judgment. The stack shows where that responsibility has to re-enter the process.

Interactive Lab

Model

The model produces language or classifications.

A model is the underlying system that generates, classifies, summarizes, or transforms text. It does not decide your matter workflow. It does not know your risk tolerance. It responds to inputs.

How It Shows Up

Day-to-day example

A lawyer asks a tool to summarize a draft contract. The model generates the summary. The tool provides the interface. The workflow decides whether the summary becomes a matter note. Governance decides whether client information can enter the system and who reviews the result.

Law firm lens

A firm pilot can fail if the policy approves only a tool name. A usable approval defines permitted matters, users, data limits, review obligations, and how output can reach a client-facing document.

In-house lens

A legal department moves faster when business users know which intake questions can use automation and which answers still need counsel. The workflow matters as much as the model.
